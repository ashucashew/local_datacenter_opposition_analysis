# Can Data Center Opposition Slow Frontier AI?

## Introduction (rough draft)

Some in the AI safety community argue that frontier AI development should slow down until safety and governance catch up.[^1] One tempting route runs through physical infrastructure. Frontier progress has tracked compute: the training compute behind frontier models has grown roughly four- to fivefold a year,[^2] and that compute needs data centers. At the same time, local opposition to data centers has surged. Data Center Watch counts at least 75 US projects, worth about $130 billion, blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone,[^3] and in November 2026 voters backed restrictions in nearly every one of roughly 41 local ballot measures.[^4] If AI progress runs through data centers, and data centers are increasingly hard to build, could riding this wave of opposition help pace the frontier?

This essay argues that it cannot. US data center opposition is an unreliable and poorly aimed lever, for three reasons. First, it mostly costs developers time and money, not capacity: developers hold backup sites, the value of capacity far exceeds the costs opposition imposes, and chip supply, not buildings, is increasingly the binding constraint. Second, the coalition's grievances, such as higher electricity bills, noise and secrecy, can be met in ways that do not slow AI, so it is not a brake anyone can steer. Third, its effects fall on the wrong part of the system. The best-funded frontier labs are the last to be squeezed, while targeted compute-governance tools aim at the actual risk.
