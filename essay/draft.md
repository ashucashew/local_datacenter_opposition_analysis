# Can Data Center Opposition Slow Frontier AI?

Some prominent voices in AI safety argue that frontier AI development should slow until safety and governance catch up.[^1] One possible brake is physical. Frontier progress has been driven by compute (the training compute behind frontier models has grown roughly four- to fivefold a year)[^2], and that compute lives in data centers. Meanwhile, local opposition to data centers in the US is surging: Data Center Watch counts at least 75 projects, worth about $130 billion, blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone.[^3] If AI progress runs through data centers, and data centers are getting harder to build, could riding this wave of opposition help pace the frontier?

For the most part, no. US data center opposition is an unreliable and poorly aimed safety lever, for two reasons. First, it costs developers time and money, not compute: capacity is worth far more than what opposition costs, developers have fallback options, and opponents' grievances can largely be paid down. Second, even if it did tighten compute, it would hit the wrong part of the system, because the best-funded frontier labs would be the last to be squeezed.

## Opposition costs time, not compute

Start with the developers' incentives. Data center capacity is extremely valuable: leases run about $30–50 billion per gigawatt over 15–20 years, or roughly $1.5–3.3 billion per gigawatt per year.[^4] Each year a 1 GW campus is delayed pushes that revenue back and leaves its tenant without compute it needs, so developers will pay a premium for speed. Generating power on site, rather than waiting years for a grid connection, costs about $100–165 per megawatt-hour, against $90–95 from the grid.[^5] By a rough calculation, that premium adds $50–650 million a year for a 1 GW campus running around the clock, a fraction of a year's lease revenue.[^6]

Opposition does impose real delays. Where they can be measured, they run about eight to fifteen months. Stokes County, North Carolina, re-approved a campus about eight months after a lawsuit voided its rezoning; Hermantown, Minnesota, approved Google's project about a year after the developer paused; and Coweta County, Georgia, took fifteen months, including a six-month moratorium, to approve Prologis's $17 billion Project Sail.[^7] But headline counts of "blocked" projects mostly measure the top of a development funnel in which most proposals never get built, opposition or not. Roughly 700–780 GW of US capacity has been publicly proposed; about 430 GW is in active pipelines; about 200 GW has signed utility or construction agreements; 60–80 GW is under construction; and 25–40 GW is delivered each year.[^8] Developers expect to lose many sites at the first local-approval step, and that is where most losses in Data Center Watch's own coverage occur. Of the 42 blocked or delayed projects named in its free briefings, only three had a publicly named end user.[^9] A lost site is usually a lost option, not lost capacity. Meanwhile, the industry keeps accelerating: Wood Mackenzie reports US capacity under construction rising from about 44 GW at the end of March 2026 to nearly 80 GW three months later.[^10]

What matters for AI progress is not buildings but compute, and the ceiling on compute is increasingly chips. A rough estimate puts the power needed for AI chips shipped in 2027 at 30–45 GW worldwide, against SemiAnalysis's forecast of 43–51 GW of new data center capacity.[^11] SemiAnalysis expects memory and logic chips to become the binding constraint in 2027–28.[^12] The estimates are uncertain and the ranges overlap, but if data centers keep pace with chip supply, opposition delays empty buildings rather than compute.

## Even a stronger backlash has workarounds

Most of today's in-flight projects cleared local approval before the backlash peaked. Could much stronger opposition push the industry off pace? Probably not: developers have many fallback options, and not every developer needs to succeed.

First, developers route around opposition. Tract lost sites in Mooresville, North Carolina, and Hanover County, Virginia, while continuing to build its 1,200-acre campus near Ashland, Virginia. Prologis was denied in Kosciusko County, Indiana, a year before winning approval for Project Sail.[^13] Projects blocked locally often proceed another way: through a lawsuit and settlement (Saline Township, Michigan, now under construction for OpenAI and Oracle), through a different government (Project Blue, approved by Pima County, Arizona, after Tucson said no), or simply after a delay (Hermantown).[^14] Where opposition concentrates in populated areas, developers move to remote land and make their own power. Amazon's GW Ranch in Pecos County, Texas, will run on 7.65 GW of its own gas generation, "off-grid initially,"[^15] and SemiAnalysis expects more than 40 GW of US data centers to use on-site power by 2028.[^16]

Second, developers and regulators can address the grievances directly. Opponents want lower bills, less noise, protected water and fair payment. Kansas and Oregon have adopted rules that make large data centers pay their own grid costs; Microsoft has pledged to pay rates covering its infrastructure; and developers offer sizable community-benefit packages, from about $14 million in Saline's settlement to $165 million proposed in Hazle Township, Pennsylvania.[^17] These measures don't always win the vote (Hanover rejected Tract despite a $15 million water offer), and some problems persist, such as low-frequency hum that falls outside standard noise rules.[^18] But every fix reduces opposition without reducing compute. That makes opposition a brake no one can steer: it weakens as developers pay their way through, whatever safety advocates want.

Third, not every developer needs to succeed. The failures in our data are smaller players: Energy Storage Solutions lost Tarboro, North Carolina, and then abandoned a nearby site; Karis Critical was denied in Naperville and withdrew in Hoffman Estates, Illinois.[^19] With roughly ten times more capacity in the pipeline than is delivered each year, the industry needs only enough sites to house the chips being made, and larger developers can absorb the demand.

Finally, if routes narrowed everywhere, Washington has strong reasons and some tools to keep the buildout going. It frames AI infrastructure as part of its competition with China.[^20] Without new legislation, it can open federal land to data centers and approve more capacity abroad: in July 2026 the Commerce Department gave approved UAE entities license-free access to advanced chips.[^21] Overriding state and local rules would require Congress. That is legally possible, as with gas pipelines and cell towers, but politically hard: the bills now before Congress move the other way, toward protecting local control.[^22]

Compute gains, then, are unlikely to be slowed much by opposition. Capacity is worth far more than the workarounds cost, developers have many fallback options, chips increasingly set the limit, and federal policy backs the buildout.

## Aimed at the wrong target

Suppose all of this failed and compute did tighten. Opposition would still be a poor safety lever, because the squeeze would fall last on the actors safety advocates worry about most.

Frontier labs can outbid everyone. The largest frontier campuses reach a gigawatt or a few, against 25–40 GW of new US capacity each year, and OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google, Microsoft and Amazon are all in the market for gigawatt-scale leases.[^23] When sites are scarce, they go to the highest bidders. Smaller developers, inference providers and academic users would feel the shortage first, so a blunt brake could concentrate frontier compute among fewer, better-funded actors. It could also push compute abroad: the UAE backstop described above would move frontier clusters to a jurisdiction with weaker oversight.

Nor is compute the only driver of progress. Algorithmic efficiency improves roughly threefold a year by Epoch's estimate,[^24] and reinforcement learning increasingly generates its own training data through rollouts, which, like inference, can run across many smaller sites.[^25] And if timelines are short, opposition cannot touch the capacity that matters. Moratoriums freeze new applications rather than revoke approvals, so capacity already under construction or permitted (by SemiAnalysis's estimate, all of 2026's and most of 2027's) is out of reach.[^26]

There is one narrow area of shared interest. Secrecy is among the most common grievances in our data: residents often learn late who a project is for and how much power and water it will use. In St. Charles, Missouri, residents faced nondisclosure agreements and an unnamed end user; Hermantown officials met with Google for more than a year before the project became public; Howell Township, Michigan, was told only of a "Fortune 100" company.[^27] Requiring developers to disclose the operator, end user and scale when they apply would help residents, and would modestly help safety by making large clusters visible. Beyond that, safety advocates would do better to focus on tools aimed at the actual risk: compute thresholds and reporting for large training runs, know-your-customer rules for compute providers, and export controls.[^28]

## Conclusion

US data center opposition is not an effective AI safety lever. Developers have fallback options and can pay down opposition's grievances; the industry needs only enough capacity to house the chips being made; and federal policy backs the buildout. Even if compute did tighten, frontier labs would be best placed to secure it, and progress would continue through better algorithms and data.

Safety advocates should not invest in data center opposition as a brake. Their effort is better spent on targeted compute governance, cooperating with local opponents only where interests genuinely coincide, as on disclosure, and without tying the safety case to zoning fights. Backlash is a real force on where compute is built and who can afford to build it, but not on whether frontier AI advances.

*Methods and limitations.* The project evidence comes from 21 large blocked or delayed projects, hand-coded from public sources, and 68 projects named in Data Center Watch's free weekly briefings (February 2025–July 2026). The sample is biased toward large, publicized fights and is suggestive rather than representative. Paywalled analyses (SemiAnalysis, Data Center Watch's full reports) were used only in their public portions, and Data Center Watch does not publish its definitions of "blocked" or "delayed."
