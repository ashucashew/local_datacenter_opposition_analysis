# Can Data Center Opposition Slow Frontier AI?

Prominent figures in the AI safety community favor slowing frontier AI development until safety and governance catch up.[^1] One way to do so runs through physical infrastructure. A main driver of frontier AI progress is compute (the training compute behind frontier models has grown roughly four- to fivefold a year),[^2] and that compute lives in data centers. Meanwhile, local opposition to data centers in the US has been surging: Data Center Watch counts at least 75 US projects, worth about $130 billion, blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026 alone.[^3] So if AI progress requires data centers, and data centers are getting harder to build, could riding this wave of opposition be a good way to pace the frontier?

For the most part, no. US data center opposition is an unreliable and poorly aimed AI safety lever, for two main reasons. First, it mostly costs developers time and money, not compute: building more data center capacity is worth far more than what addressing or routing around opposition costs. Second, even if it did tighten compute, it would hit the wrong part of the system, because the best-funded frontier labs would be the last to be squeezed.

## Opposition costs time, not compute

Start with the incentives for data center developers. Data center capacity is extremely valuable right now, with leases worth about $30–50 billion per gigawatt over 15–20 years, or roughly $1.5–3.3 billion per gigawatt per year.[^4] Bringing a 1 GW data center online a year earlier brings forward that much lease revenue, so it is worth paying a premium to build quickly. Generating power on site, rather than waiting years for a grid connection, costs about $100–165 per megawatt-hour, against $90–95 from the grid.[^5] A back-of-the-envelope calculation puts the extra cost of a year of off-grid power for a 1 GW campus at about $50–650 million, a fraction of a year's lease revenue.[^6]

To be fair, opposition does impose real delays. Where they can be measured, they run about eight to fifteen months. Stokes County, North Carolina, re-approved a campus about eight months after a lawsuit voided its rezoning; Hermantown, Minnesota, approved Google's project about a year after the developer paused; and Coweta County, Georgia, took fifteen months, including a six-month moratorium, to approve Prologis's $17 billion Project Sail.[^7] But headline counts of "blocked" projects measure losses at the top of a development funnel. Roughly 700–780 GW of US capacity has been publicly proposed; about 430 GW is in active pipelines; about 200 GW has signed utility or construction agreements; 60–80 GW is under construction; and 25–40 GW is delivered each year.[^8] Projects drop out for many reasons: no tenant, no grid connection, no financing and, increasingly, local opposition. More opposition will kill more sites. But because developers propose far more capacity than tenants and chips can fill, demand from a lost site can usually move to another. A lost site is usually a lost option, not lost capacity. Opposition slows the buildout only if it destroys that surplus faster than developers can replace it.

Meanwhile, the industry as a whole keeps accelerating. Wood Mackenzie reports US capacity under construction rising from about 44 GW at the end of March 2026 to nearly 80 GW three months later.[^9] By my rough estimate, the AI chips shipped worldwide in 2027 will need 30–45 GW of new capacity, against SemiAnalysis's forecast of 43–51 GW of new data center capacity worldwide.[^10] The estimates are uncertain and the ranges overlap, but if data centers keep pace with chip supply, opposition delays empty buildings rather than compute.

## Even a stronger backlash has workarounds

Most of today's in-flight projects cleared local approval before the backlash peaked. The question, then, is whether far stronger opposition could push the industry off pace. Probably not: developers have many workarounds, and the federal government is likely on their side.

For one, developers can route around opposition. Projects blocked locally often proceed another way: through a lawsuit and settlement (Saline Township, Michigan, now under construction for OpenAI and Oracle), through a different government (Project Blue, approved by Pima County, Arizona, after Tucson said no), or simply after a delay (Hermantown).[^11] And where opposition is high in populated areas, developers move to remote land and make their own power. Amazon's GW Ranch in Pecos County, Texas, will run on 7.65 GW of its own gas generation, "off-grid initially,"[^12] and SemiAnalysis expects more than 40 GW of US data centers to use on-site power by 2028.[^13]

If developers can't route around opposition, they can address opponents' grievances directly. Kansas and Oregon have adopted rules that make large data centers pay their own grid costs; Microsoft has pledged to pay rates covering its infrastructure; and developers offer sizable community-benefit packages, from about $14 million in Saline Township's settlement to $165 million proposed in Hazle Township, Pennsylvania.[^14] These measures don't always win the vote (Hanover County, Virginia, rejected Tract's project despite a $15 million water offer), and some problems persist, such as low-frequency hum that falls outside standard noise rules.[^15] But every fix reduces opposition without reducing compute, which is what matters for pacing the AI frontier.

And if opposition grew strong enough that routes narrowed everywhere, Washington has a strong incentive to keep the buildout going: it treats AI infrastructure as central to maintaining a lead over China.[^16] Even without new legislation, it can open federal land to data centers and approve more capacity abroad. In July 2026, the Commerce Department gave approved UAE entities license-free access to advanced chips.[^17]

Compute gains, then, are unlikely to be slowed much by opposition. Developers have many backup options, the value of capacity dwarfs the cost of workarounds, and federal policy backs the buildout and has every incentive to keep backing it.

## Aimed at the wrong target

Now suppose everything above failed: backup options didn't work out, opponents' grievances couldn't be bought off, and Washington didn't step in. One problem would remain. Even if compute capacity did tighten, frontier AI progress would likely continue, and with it the risks safety advocates worry about.

Frontier labs, among the best-funded companies in the world, can outbid everyone for compute. OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google, Microsoft and Amazon are all in the market for gigawatt-scale leases,[^18] and they have the money to outcompete smaller developers, inference providers and academic users for whatever capacity remains.

Even if frontier labs couldn't secure as much compute as they wanted, frontier progress would continue. Compute is only one of three pillars of AI progress; the other two, algorithms and data, could keep driving progress even with compute held constant. Algorithmic efficiency improves roughly threefold a year by Epoch's estimate (other estimates range from two- to sixfold),[^19] and reinforcement learning generates much of its own training data through rollouts that, like inference, can run across many smaller sites.[^20] Gains also compound: more efficient models let researchers run more experiments on the same compute budget.

Finally, a data center brake would apply only in the US, while the frontier is global. China already has about 24 GW of operating data center capacity and roughly 50 GW more planned or under construction,[^21] and the rest of the world is growing too. A US-only brake can't pace a global frontier, and it may shift that frontier toward China.

Data center opposition, then, is not an effective lever for pacing the frontier. There is, however, a narrow shared interest. Secrecy is one of the most common grievances among data center opponents: residents often don't learn who a project is for, or how much power and water it will use, until late in the process. St. Charles, Missouri, faced nondisclosure agreements and an unnamed end user; Hermantown officials met with Google for more than a year before the project was made public, and residents sued over closed meetings; Howell Township, Michigan, was told only of a "Fortune 100" company; and in Indianapolis's Franklin Township, a city councilor had signed a nondisclosure agreement.[^22] Requiring developers to disclose the operator, end user and scale of a project when they apply would help residents. It would also modestly help safety, by making large clusters visible.

Beyond that, the safety policy community would do better to focus on tools that target the actual risk, such as compute thresholds and reporting, know-your-customer rules for compute providers, and export controls.[^23] Joining the data center backlash could also invite accusations of astroturfing, which could weaken the AI safety community's standing in Washington.

## Conclusion

US data center opposition is not an effective AI safety lever. Developers have fallback options and can pay down opponents' grievances, the industry needs only enough capacity to house the chips being made, and federal policy backs the buildout. Even if compute did tighten, frontier labs would be best placed to secure it, and progress would continue through better algorithms and data.

Safety advocates should not invest in data center opposition as a brake. Their effort is better spent on targeted compute governance, cooperating with local opponents only where interests genuinely coincide, as on disclosure, and without tying the safety case to zoning fights. Backlash is a real force on where compute is built and who can afford to build it, but not on whether frontier AI advances.

*A note on sources and limitations.* The case examples come from a review of about 50 large contested projects and the 68 projects named in Data Center Watch's free weekly briefings (February 2025–July 2026), built from news coverage, local government records and developer statements. They illustrate how developers respond to opposition; they are not a representative sample, and they lean toward large, well-publicized fights. Capacity, pipeline and chip figures come from industry trackers and forecasts that use different definitions and often disagree, so they are presented as rough ranges. SemiAnalysis and Data Center Watch were used only in their public portions, and Data Center Watch does not publish its definitions of "blocked" or "delayed."
